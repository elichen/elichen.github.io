This page runs the whole-brain model of The Whole Fly Brain on a different connectome. BANC maps the brain and the ventral nerve cord of one fly: 162,733 neurons and 42.2 million synapses [1]. It runs in your browser with a simulated fly in flight. The brain model steers the fly. It does not move the wings. A trained policy moves them.

Why a dodge

In living flies, Croke et al. recorded DNp03, a pair of descending neurons. It fires more to a looming object on its own side. If you activate one DNp03 in a tethered fly in flight, the wingbeat on its side gets larger, the other gets smaller, and the head turns away. Its main inputs are the visual neurons LPLC1, LPLC4 and LC4 [2]. So this is a test of the model with a clear answer from real flies.

The model passes it (tools/loom.py). Each looming set gets random input at 100 Hz. Firing of DNp03, left cell / right cell:

InputDNp03 left / right
Left looming neurons169 / 0 Hz
Right looming neurons0 / 175 Hz
Both165 / 170 Hz

The nerve cord also agrees, at the level of motor neurons (tools/wing_turn.py). One DNp03 at 200 Hz makes the motor neurons of b3 and i1 fire on the other side only (b3 22–34 Hz, i1 22–31 Hz, 0 on its own side). Croke et al. list both as muscles that reduce wingbeat amplitude, and they predict this pathway from the connectome. But they also predict that DNp03 increases the wingbeat on its own side through b1, and b1 stays silent in the model.

This does not work for smell. In the same model on FlyWire, olfactory input ignites the whole brain, and the steering neurons do not follow the side of the odor (see Can it walk?). The visual input here stays local: 30,000–110,000 spikes per second.

The test

We aim the object at the place where the fly will be if it flies straight on, so a fly that does not turn is hit. The fly flies at 14 cm/s. The object is a sphere of radius 1 cm, and it starts 12 cm away. Ten runs for each row, half from each side (tools/dodge.mjs). "Clearance" is the smallest distance from the head to the object's surface, in cm. A value at or below 0 is a hit. A fly that does not turn is hit in all 80 runs.

Turning away is better only when the object comes from the side or from behind. From the front, the turn takes the fly along the object's path, and the control that turns toward the object does better. At 45 cm/s from the front, the object arrives about 0.15 s after the saccade starts, and the fly is hit every time. The buttons on this page send the object from 75° at 30 cm/s. Any turn can make a miss, because the object is aimed at a straight flight, so the clearance says more than the hits. The eye rule and the threshold are our choices, so these distances are not a prediction for a real fly.

The parts that are not the connectome

The eye. The model has no photoreceptors here. We compute how fast the object's angular size grows. That sets the input rate of the LC4, LPLC1 and LPLC4 neurons on the object's side, up to 100 Hz at 6 rad/s.

The turn. When DNp03 left minus right passes 60 Hz, which takes several spikes within about 50 ms, the fly makes one saccade away from the active side. The next one can start 0.3 s later. So the brain decides whether and which way, and only that. The shape is fixed: it comes from 44 saccades of real flies in flybody's data, a peak of 43 rad/s and a width of 40 ms at half height, with 27° of roll into the turn. In those flies, the median heading change is 81°; ours is about 100°. The direction is from Croke et al. We use only DNp03. Real flies use many descending neurons.

The wings. Vaxenburg et al. trained a network with reinforcement learning to copy the flight of real flies [3]. It runs at 5 kHz, and physics runs at 20 kHz. It plays a recorded wingbeat at about 218 Hz with small corrections, and follows a path that we make from a speed and a turn rate. Our port gives the same actions as flybody's for the same input, to 5 parts in 10 million (tools/flight_parity.mjs). The wings are rigid, because flybody does not simulate wing flexion.

Speed and pose. The fly flies at 14 cm/s, the median speed in flybody's recordings. Its thorax is about 45° nose up, the pose the policy was trained on, near the published body angle of about 47.5° for hovering flies [4]. The abdomen hangs lower, at about 72°, so the fly looks upright. This surprised us too, but many photos of real flies in free flight look like this, for example this composite by Floris van Breugel. Other photos show a flatter body, and we judged this by eye: we have not measured the abdomen of a real fly in flight. Real flies get flatter at higher speed, but the policy cannot fly that fast: at 30 cm/s it does not hold a steady pose, and a saccade makes it fall.

The legs. flybody fixes the legs in a tucked pose in flight, which its authors estimated by eye. Photos show them hanging lower, so we moved them 40% of the way toward the model's zero pose. This is also by eye. The policy still flies with them there.

What you see. A screen shows about 60 frames per second and the wings beat 218 times per second, so one frame cannot show a wingbeat. The wings on this page are the real poses of the last wingbeat, drawn faintly over each other, as a slow camera shutter shows them. Press Slow motion to see each beat at 1/50 speed.

What it cannot do

  • Take off or land. The fly starts in the air. The giant fiber, which drives the jump, reaches its motor neuron mainly through an electrical synapse, and the connectome has none.
  • Make its own wingbeat. We do not know how to turn the firing rates of the wing motor neurons into wing motion, and we did not try.
  • Feel its own flight. The halteres and wings send no input to the model. Real flight depends on them.
