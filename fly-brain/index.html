flybody (Vaxenburg et al. 2025), with its trained walking and flight policies

One fly lives on a kitchen table, with one body and one connectome. The fly gets hungry and goes to sugar: it walks if the sugar is near and flies if the sugar is far. It lands, eats, gets bored and goes to a different place. This is not a whole fly made from the connectome. The brain model makes some of the decisions. Trained policies move the body, and stated rules do the rest.

Takeoff and landing, with no new policy

flybody's fly walks with one policy and flies with another. Neither policy learned to change to the other. We first trained a takeoff policy with PPO on 2,048 flies on a GPU. This failed: after about 10.6 million steps, no fly reached flight, and each episode ended after about 0.11 s. We stopped the training.

Then we asked a simpler question: how much can the unchanged flight policy recover from? We started a fly in the air at zero speed, with its thorax at different angles. Then we told the policy to change gradually to cruise (tools/catch.mjs).

So the legs have one job: lift the nose. Before a jump, a real fly sets its posture [4]. Our fly reaches with the front legs and crouches on the hind legs for 0.1 s with its wings shut, which gives about 20–28°. Then the flight policy starts the wings, on a path that begins at the fly's own pose, while the middle and hind legs push. If the nose is not up by 12°, the fly does not jump. We found these numbers by random search.

To land, the path falls to 0.8 cm at 4 cm/s and slows to a hover with the nose at 35°. The path then waits until the fly is almost still, and sinks. At the first touch of a foot, the wings shut and the walking policy takes over. Stillness matters: a touch at 2.5 cm/s or more often tipped the fly over. The rate of fall also matters. From 11 cm at 8 cm/s, the flight policy lost control in 7 of 9 descents, and at 3–5 cm/s in none of 28.

One more problem took time to find. A rotation has two equal descriptions, q and −q, but a policy reads the numbers. When the heading of the fly passed 180°, the sign could disagree with the path, and the policy turned the fly onto its back. Now the signs agree at each change from one policy to the other.

Can you swat it?

Often, yes. A swatter with a half-width of 3 cm starts 30 cm away and aims at a standing fly. Each row has 40 trials, and more where a row combines speeds (SWATTEST=20 node tools/day.mjs body, two seeds). With the jump turned off, all 240 control trials are hits. The page only counts a hit, and a hit does not hurt the fly. The buttons on this page use 30 cm/s.

A real fly has a slow voluntary takeoff and a fast escape that the giant fiber can start within milliseconds [5]. Our fly also has two: its usual rear-up takes 0.1 s, and the rear-up from the giant fiber takes 35 ms. With the slow rear-up, 32 of 40 swats from the right at 30 cm/s were hits, and with the fast one, 4.

We use the fast rear-up only for the escape: with it on every takeoff, the fly canceled about 5 times as many takeoffs. We chose the 35 ms from a short search, not from a recording. A fly that jumps often also falls often: at one swatter per 30 s, we put the fly back on its feet 7 to 10 times in five minutes.

The sides differ, and we now think that the unusual side is the right side. The left eye often does not make the fly jump. One neuron explains most of this. PVLP010 inhibits the giant fiber on its own side: 132 synapses on the left in BANC, and only 41 on the right. In FlyWire it has 113 and 137. Left PVLP010 also has more than twice the input of the right (5,344 synapses against 2,335). In a test without adaptation and with left looming input, we silenced left PVLP010, and the left giant fiber rose from 12 to 48 Hz.

So the right PVLP010 of this one fly, or its reconstruction, is the unusual one. This makes the jump from the right eye unusually reliable. On FlyWire, the same input to one eye drives the giant fiber only weakly, from either eye (0–14 Hz).

An earlier version of this page said that the cause was the incomplete BANC annotation of the left optic lobe. That was wrong: our input goes directly to the looming neurons and does not go through the optic lobe.

Why it dodges

In living flies, Croke et al. recorded DNp03, a pair of descending neurons. It fires more to a looming object on its own side. Activation of one DNp03 in a tethered flying fly makes the wingbeat on its side larger and the other smaller, and the head turns away. Its main inputs are the visual neurons LPLC1, LPLC4 and LC4 [15].

The model passes this test (tools/loom.py). Left looming neurons at 100 Hz make DNp03 fire at 159 Hz on the left and 0 on the right. Right looming neurons give 0 and 168 Hz. In the nerve cord, one DNp03 makes the motor neurons of the wing muscles b3 and i1 fire on the other side only. Croke et al. predict this. They also predict b1 on the same side, and b1 stays silent in the model (tools/wing_turn.py).

When DNp03 left minus right passes 60 Hz, the fly makes one saccade away from the active side. The brain model chooses whether to turn and in which direction. The shape of the saccade is fixed and comes from 44 saccades of real flies in flybody's data. It has a peak of 43 rad/s, a width of 40 ms and 27° of roll into the turn.

We tested this in an earlier scene with the fly always in the air and contacts off (tools/dodge.mjs at commit 80772509). The object aimed at the point where a straight flight would go, so the object hit a fly with no turn in all 80 runs. Each row has ten runs. A turn away is better only when the object comes from the side or from behind. From the front, the turn takes the fly along the path of the object. That test used a less sensitive eye rule than this page (full input at 6 rad/s, here 2 rad/s). We chose the eye rule and the threshold, so these distances are not a prediction for a real fly.

The fly flies at 14 cm/s, the median speed in flybody's recordings. At 30 cm/s the policy does not hold a steady pose, and a saccade makes the fly fall. The thorax of the fly is about 45° nose up, as trained, near the published body angle for flies that hover [17]. It looks upright. We judged the abdomen and the hanging legs by eye from photos.

The wings beat at about 218 Hz, too fast for a screen, so the page draws the real poses of the last wingbeat together, faintly.

Turn on Sound to hear the wings. The sound comes from the same wingbeat, not from a recording. The force on a wing grows with the square of its speed, and the ear gets the rate of change of that force. One wingbeat of the policy's wing pattern gives the wave, and the policy's wing frequency gives the pitch.

We chose the shares of lift and drag and the volume, and we did not compare the sound with a recording of a fly. The volume falls as you zoom out. In slow motion the sound stops, because 4 Hz is too low to hear. When the model runs slower than real time, the pitch stays at the real wing frequency.

A day, in numbers

We made six runs of five simulated minutes with no swatter (node tools/day.mjs body 300). A run has about 10 flights, 7 meals, and 2 takeoffs that the fly canceled because the nose was not up. In each run we also had to put the fly back on its feet: 3 times on average, from 1 to 6.

When the fly falls on its back, it cannot stand up again. Neither policy learned this, so after 0.4 s we put the fly back on its feet, and the page counts each time. Of those 18 falls, 11 were rear-ups that tipped over, 5 happened in flight and 2 on the way down. We tried to stop a rear-up when it started to tip, but this did not help: the fly fell anyway.

The brain model

Each neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit [6]. Its potential decays to rest, and synaptic input pushes it up or down. At threshold it fires, resets, and ignores input for a short time.

Every neuron has the same values: rest and reset −52 mV, threshold −45 mV, membrane time constant 20 ms. The other shared values are synaptic time constant 5 ms, refractory period 2.2 ms and delay 1.8 ms.

The connectome gives the remaining values. The weight of a connection is its number of synapses times 0.275 mV, and a predicted transmitter gives its sign [9]. GABA, glutamate and histamine inhibit. The model at rest stays at rest until input arrives. It does not always go back to rest when the input stops (see What is wrong with this fly).

Two changes from Shiu et al., for realism. Each change makes the model more like a fly, and neither makes it complete. We found both while we looked for the cause of a failure. With the original rule, the model on BANC does not go back to rest after P9 or looming input (figure).

Serotonin. Their rule gives every transmitter other than GABA and glutamate a fast excitatory effect. The serotonin receptors that we found in flies are slow G-protein types, so on BANC we give serotonin neurons no synapses at all. With fast excitation, a loop of seven neurons (figure) kept the model firing at about 31,000 spikes per second, with no input, after any walk. One spike across about 162 synapses makes the next neuron fire, and this loop has connections of 281–339. Real serotonin has slow effects, and the model has none.

The labels are predictions, and nobody has verified them for these neurons. FlyWire predicts glutamate for LAL120a, LAL120b and LAL122, which would make them inhibitory. We think that the BANC prediction is more likely for LAL120a and LAL120b. BANC is right for 48 of 51 neurons of known serotonin types, and FlyWire for 9 of 61. LAL125 and LAL108 are known serotonin neurons nearby, and they show the same disagreement. LAL122 is less clear.

With either label, the fast excitatory loop is wrong. Dopamine, octopamine and tyramine stay excitatory, because the BANC dopamine predictions on large neurons are mostly wrong.

Adaptation. Real neurons fire less when they keep firing. Shiu's model has no such effect. In our model, each spike raises the threshold of the neuron by 0.05 mV, and the rise decays with a time constant of 200 ms. At 150 Hz that is about 1.5 mV of the 7 mV to threshold. Without adaptation, looming input leaves the model firing for several seconds in 5 of 6 runs, and the giant fiber also keeps firing.

With adaptation, the model stopped in all 192 runs across P9, P9 with sugar and looming input, 1 s and 5 s long. Neither change alone is enough: with adaptation and the original rule for serotonin, the model still kept firing in 3 of 4 runs.

We chose the size and the time constant from a sweep, not from recordings. At 0.1 mV, MN9 under sugar falls by a quarter, and at 0.5 mV, by about three quarters. So the weakest value that works is the right one. Adaptation does not fix everything: on FlyWire, odor input still causes runaway firing with adaptation on.

The numbers on FlyWire below use the original model, with neither change.

The model runs in WebAssembly, compiled from Rust, in steps of 0.1 ms. The engine does not step a neuron that cannot reach threshold without new input. When input arrives, one calculation brings the neuron up to date, so the result does not change.

We checked the engine against Shiu et al.'s Brian 2 model [11], on the brain-only FlyWire connectome (version 783, 138,639 neurons [7][8]). The paper used version 630. With the same random input in ten runs of 1 s, Brian and the engine made the same 1,999,953 spikes, each in the same step.

We also made 30 runs of sugar with separate random input. All 437 neurons that fired agree within 3 standard errors, and MN9 fired at 78.6 Hz in Brian and 78.2 Hz in the engine. We did not check BANC against Brian. It is the same engine on a different connectome, with the same weight per synapse.

Stop on sugar. Sapkal et al. found that sugar stops walking in real flies, and showed why in this model [2]. We repeated it on FlyWire (tools/intent.py). With P9 at 100 Hz, oDN1 fires at 13.4 Hz. Sugar at 50, 100 and 150 Hz decreases this to 9.5, 2.8 and 1.0 Hz. Bitter at 150 Hz does not (13.8 Hz).

From firing to the mouth. McKellar et al. recorded what each proboscis muscle does [16], and BANC names their motor neurons. The page knows which neurons move which joint: MN9 and MN1, MN2 for the rostrum, MN4 and MN3 for the haustellum, MN7 for the labellum. No recording gives the force for a firing rate, so we chose a rule. Activation is 1 − e−r/40 Hz of the mean rate over 30 ms.

Runaway firing. On FlyWire, we activate 12 LB3b taste neurons on the fly's right at 150 Hz. The firing spreads through excitatory local neurons of the antennal lobe until about 8,400 neurons fire, among them about 3,400 Kenyon cells. Brian does the same. We do not know which missing part of the model causes this.

Shiu et al. list the limits of the model [6]. All neurons have the same parameters, and many real fly neurons do not fire spikes. The model has no gap junctions, neuropeptides, neuromodulation or internal state. Transmitter predictions can be wrong. Neurons have no activity at rest, so inhibition of a silent neuron has no effect. Absolute firing rates are not reliable, and changes in rate are more reliable.

Can it walk by itself?

No. A trained policy moves the legs on this page. We activated DNg100, a descending neuron that starts walking in real flies, at 50–200 Hz on BANC. About 80–110 of the 393 leg motor neurons fire, with no rhythm, and flexors and extensors of a joint fire together. A leg would become stiff and not step.

We then activated each of the 473 types of descending neuron in turn. 169 types make leg motor neurons fire. None makes a rhythm clearly above random firing, and none makes flexors and extensors alternate.

Pugliese et al. found a walking rhythm in a rate model of the nerve cord [12]. We wrote their model again from their Methods and ran it on BANC (tools/rate_vnc.py). Their result holds. With DNg100 driven at a moderate input, the networks of all six legs make a rhythm at 8–11 Hz in 8 of 8 runs. Most runs stop with stronger input.

We sent this rhythm to the legs of a tethered flybody fly (tools/leg_drive.py). Most feet move by less than 0.1 mm, one hind foot rises 0.27 mm, and the middle legs almost do not move.

Real flies give a clear test. Sapkal et al. activated DNg100 in flies with no head and little leg feedback. All six legs stepped, left and right alternated, and the legs kept a tripod [13]. The model on BANC does not do this (tools/phase.py, input 90).

Muscles that swing a front leg forward and back are about 45–75° apart, not 180°. The front legs are within about 35° of each other, not 180°. We tested the input that gives the clearest rhythm, and there is no tripod. So the connectome holds part of what walking needs, but this model does not walk from it.

Smell fails too. A real fly turns toward the antenna that smells more, through DNa02 [14]. On FlyWire, odor input usually causes runaway firing across the whole brain or does nothing. DNa02 fires on the fly's left for input to either antenna (tools/intent.py steer).

We silenced Kenyon cells, excitatory antennal lobe neurons or all neuromodulator neurons, and we weakened all synapses. These changes did not help, and adaptation does not help either. This may be an error in the model, not in the fly.

The jump does not reach the muscles. Looming neurons drive the giant fiber, but the giant fiber does not drive the motor neuron of the jump muscle. In the fly that synapse is mostly electrical, and the connectome has chemical synapses only. So a threshold on the firing rate of the giant fiber starts the jump.

What is wrong with this fly

  • It smells nothing and sees nothing. Odor input causes runaway firing in the model (see Can it walk by itself?). Visual input from photoreceptors does not reach the looming neurons in this model (tools/vision_reach.py). The fly knows where the sugar is because we tell it.
  • The brain does not control the landing. We looked for a landing command. LPLC4 drives DNp07, but about the same from one eye (67 / 0 Hz) as from two (74 / 74 Hz). That does not pass the test that we set in advance (tools/land.py). So a rule decides.
  • Hunger is one number. The fly has no thirst, sleep, mating, grooming or learning.
  • The world is flat. The fly cannot walk on the fruit or land on the mug, because the walking policy knows only flat ground. Its goals and paths go around what stands there. The fly sometimes goes through an edge: in those six runs, it was inside something for 1.8 s of 30 minutes.
  • The fly does not feel its own body. The legs, wings and halteres send no input to the model.

The page downloads about 45 MB. In our test (headless Chrome on an Apple M4 Mac) the page runs at about real time on the table and 0.5× in the air. The cause is that physics in the air runs at 20 kHz with every leg joint free. The clock shows the speed.
