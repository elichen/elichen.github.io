One fly, one body and one connectome live on a kitchen table. The fly gets hungry and goes to sugar, walking if the sugar is near and flying if it is far. It lands, eats, gets bored and goes somewhere else. This is not a whole fly made from the connectome. The brain model makes some of the decisions. Trained policies move the body, and stated rules do the rest.

Takeoff and landing, with no new policy

flybody's fly walks with one policy and flies with another, and neither was trained to change to the other. We first trained a takeoff policy with PPO on 2,048 flies on a GPU. It failed: after about 10.6 million steps, no fly reached flight, and each episode ended after about 0.11 s. We stopped it.

Then we asked a simpler question: how much can the flight policy recover from without any change? We started a fly in the air, with zero speed, with its thorax at different angles, and asked it to blend into cruise (tools/catch.mjs).

So the legs have one job: lift the nose. Before a jump, a real fly sets its posture [4]. Ours reaches with the front legs and crouches on the hind legs for 0.1 s with its wings shut, which gives about 20–28°. Then the flight policy starts the wings, on a path that begins at the fly's own pose, while the middle and hind legs push. If the nose is not up by 12°, the fly does not jump. We found these numbers by random search.

To land, the path falls to 0.8 cm at 4 cm/s, brakes to a hover with the nose at 35°, waits until the fly is almost still, and sinks. At the first touch of a foot, the wings shut and the walking policy takes over. Stillness matters: a touch at 2.5 cm/s or more often tipped the fly over. So does the rate of fall: from 11 cm at 8 cm/s, the flight policy lost control in 7 of 9 descents, and at 3–5 cm/s in none of 28.

One more thing took some finding. A rotation has two equal descriptions, q and −q, but a policy reads the numbers. When the fly's heading passed 180°, the sign could disagree with the path, and the policy threw the fly on its back. Each handover now makes the signs agree.

Can you swat it?

Often, yes. A swatter of 3 cm half-width starts 30 cm away and aims at a standing fly. 40 trials per row, more where a row joins speeds (SWATTEST=20 node tools/day.mjs body, two seeds). If the jump is off, all 240 control trials are hits. A hit is only counted. It does not hurt the fly. The buttons on this page use 30 cm/s.

A real fly has a slow voluntary takeoff and a fast escape that the giant fiber can start within milliseconds [5]. Ours has two too. Its usual rear-up takes 0.1 s, and the giant fiber's takes 35 ms. With the slow one, 32 of 40 swats from the right at 30 cm/s were hits, and with the fast one, 4. We use the fast one only for the escape: used for every takeoff, it called off about 5 times as many launches. The 35 ms is our choice, from a short search. It is not from a recording, and a fly that jumps often falls often: with a swatter every 30 s, the fly was put back on its feet 7 to 10 times in five minutes.

The sides differ, and we now think that the odd side is the right. The left eye often does not make the fly jump. One neuron explains most of this. PVLP010 inhibits the giant fiber on its own side: 132 synapses on the left in BANC, and only 41 on the right. In FlyWire it has 113 and 137. Left PVLP010 also has more than twice the input of the right (5,344 synapses against 2,335). In a test without adaptation, silencing left PVLP010 raised the left giant fiber from 12 to 48 Hz under left looming input. So the right PVLP010 of this one fly, or its reconstruction, is the unusual one, and that makes the right eye's jump unusually reliable. On FlyWire, the same one-eye input hardly drives the giant fiber from either eye (0–14 Hz). An earlier version of this page blamed BANC's incomplete left optic lobe annotation. That was wrong: our input goes directly to the looming neurons, past the optic lobe.

Why it dodges

In living flies, Croke et al. recorded DNp03, a pair of descending neurons. It fires more to a looming object on its own side. If you activate one DNp03 in a tethered fly in flight, the wingbeat on its side gets larger, the other gets smaller, and the head turns away. Its main inputs are the visual neurons LPLC1, LPLC4 and LC4 [15]. The model passes this test (tools/loom.py): left looming neurons at 100 Hz give DNp03 159 Hz on the left and 0 on the right, and right looming neurons give 0 and 168 Hz. In the nerve cord, one DNp03 makes the motor neurons of the wing muscles b3 and i1 fire on the other side only, as Croke et al. predict. They also predict b1 on the same side, and b1 stays silent in the model (tools/wing_turn.py).

When DNp03 left minus right passes 60 Hz, the fly makes one saccade away from the active side. The brain chooses whether and which way. The shape is fixed: from 44 saccades of real flies in flybody's data, a peak of 43 rad/s and a width of 40 ms, with 27° of roll into the turn.

We tested this in an earlier scene with the fly always in the air and contacts off (tools/dodge.mjs at commit 80772509). The object was aimed at where a straight flight would go, so a fly that does not turn was hit in all 80 runs. Ten runs per row. Clearance is the smallest distance from head to object surface, in cm.

Turning away is better only when the object comes from the side or from behind. From the front, the turn takes the fly along the object's path. That test used a less sensitive eye rule than this page (full input at 6 rad/s, here 2 rad/s). The eye rule and the threshold are our choices, so these distances are not a prediction for a real fly.

The fly flies at 14 cm/s, the median speed in flybody's recordings. At 30 cm/s the policy does not hold a steady pose, and a saccade makes it fall. Its thorax is about 45° nose up, as trained, near the published body angle for hovering flies [17]. It looks upright. We judged the abdomen and the hanging legs by eye from photos. The wings beat at about 218 Hz, too fast for a screen, so they are drawn as the real poses of the last wingbeat, laid faintly over each other.

Turn on Sound to hear the wings. The sound comes from the same wingbeat, not from a recording. The force on a wing grows with the square of its speed, and the ear gets the rate of change of that force. One beat of the policy's wing pattern gives the wave, and the policy's wing frequency gives the pitch. The shares of lift and drag and the volume are our choices, and we did not compare the sound with a recording of a fly. The volume falls as you zoom out. In slow motion it is silent, because 4 Hz is too low to hear. When the model runs slower than real time, the pitch stays at the real wing frequency.

A day, in numbers

Six runs of five simulated minutes with no swatter (node tools/day.mjs body 300). A run has about 10 flights, 7 meals, and 2 launches called off because the nose was not up. In each run we also had to put the fly back on its feet: 3 times on average, from 1 to 6. When the fly falls on its back, it cannot right itself. No policy was trained for this, so after 0.4 s we set it back on its feet, and the page counts each time. Of those 18 falls, 11 were rear-ups that tipped over, 5 happened in flight and 2 on the way down. Stopping a rear-up as soon as it starts to tip did not help: the fly fell anyway.

The brain model

Each neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit [6]. Its potential decays to rest, and synaptic input pushes it up or down. At threshold it fires, resets, and ignores input for a short time. Every neuron has the same values: rest and reset −52 mV, threshold −45 mV, membrane time constant 20 ms, synaptic time constant 5 ms, refractory period 2.2 ms, delay 1.8 ms. The connectome gives the rest. A connection's weight is its number of synapses times 0.275 mV, and its sign comes from a predicted transmitter [9]. GABA, glutamate and histamine inhibit. A brain at rest stays at rest until input arrives. It does not always go back to rest when the input stops (see What is wrong with this fly).

Two changes from Shiu et al., for realism. Each makes the model more like a fly, and neither makes it complete. We found both while we looked for a failure: with the original rule, the model on BANC does not go back to rest after P9 or looming input (figure).

Serotonin. Their rule gives every transmitter other than GABA and glutamate a fast excitatory effect. The serotonin receptors we found in flies are slow G-protein types, so on BANC we give serotonin neurons no synapses at all. As fast excitation, a loop of seven neurons (figure) kept the model firing at about 31,000 spikes per second, with no input, after any walk. One spike across about 162 synapses makes the next neuron fire, and this loop has connections of 281–339. Real serotonin has slow effects, and the model has none. The labels are predictions, and nobody has verified them for these neurons. FlyWire predicts glutamate for LAL120a, LAL120b and LAL122, which would make them inhibitory. We think BANC's prediction is more likely for LAL120a and LAL120b: BANC is right for 48 of 51 neurons of known serotonin types, FlyWire for 9 of 61, and LAL125 and LAL108, known serotonin neurons nearby, show the same disagreement. LAL122 is less clear. In both cases the fast excitatory loop is wrong. Dopamine, octopamine and tyramine stay excitatory, because BANC's dopamine predictions on large neurons are mostly wrong.

Adaptation. Real neurons fire less when they keep firing. Shiu's model has no such effect. Ours: each spike raises the neuron's own threshold by 0.05 mV, and the rise decays with a time constant of 200 ms. At 150 Hz that is about 1.5 mV of the 7 mV to threshold. Without it, looming input leaves the model firing for several seconds, in 5 of 6 runs, and the giant fiber keeps firing too. With it, the model stopped in all 192 runs across P9, P9 with sugar and looming input, 1 s and 5 s long. Each change alone is not enough: with adaptation and the original rule for serotonin, the model still kept firing in 3 of 4 runs. The size and the time constant are our choices, from a sweep, and not from recordings. At 0.1 mV, MN9 under sugar falls by a quarter, and at 0.5 mV, by about three quarters, so the weakest value that works is the right one. It does not fix everything: on FlyWire, odor input still ignites the model with adaptation on.

The numbers on FlyWire below use the original model, with neither change.

The model runs in WebAssembly, compiled from Rust, in steps of 0.1 ms. A neuron that cannot reach threshold without new input is not stepped. When input arrives, one calculation brings it up to date, so the result does not change.

We checked the engine against Shiu et al.'s model in Brian 2 [11], on the brain-only FlyWire connectome (version 783, 138,639 neurons [7][8]) that the paper used. With the same random input in ten runs of 1 s, Brian made 1,999,953 spikes, and the engine made the same spikes, each in the same step. With separate random input, 30 runs of sugar: all 437 neurons that fired agree within 3 standard errors, and MN9 fired at 78.6 Hz in Brian and 78.2 Hz in the engine. We did not check BANC against Brian. It is the same engine on a different connectome, with the same weight per synapse.

Stopping on sugar. Sapkal et al. found that sugar stops walking in real flies, and showed why in this model [2]. We repeated it on FlyWire (tools/intent.py). With P9 at 100 Hz, oDN1 fires at 13.4 Hz. Sugar at 50, 100 and 150 Hz reduces it to 9.5, 2.8 and 1.0 Hz. Bitter at 150 Hz does not (13.8 Hz).

From firing to the mouth. McKellar et al. recorded what each proboscis muscle does [16], and BANC names their motor neurons. So the page knows which neurons move which joint: MN9 and MN1, MN2 for the rostrum, MN4 and MN3 for the haustellum, MN7 for the labellum. No recording gives force for a firing rate, so we chose a rule: activation is 1 − e−r/40 Hz of the mean rate over 30 ms.

It can run away. On FlyWire, 12 LB3b taste neurons on the fly's right at 150 Hz spread through excitatory local neurons of the antennal lobe until about 8,400 neurons fire, among them about 3,400 Kenyon cells. Brian does the same. We do not know which missing part of the model causes this.

Shiu et al. list the model's limits [6]. All neurons have the same parameters, and many real fly neurons do not fire spikes. There are no gap junctions, neuropeptides, neuromodulation or internal state. Transmitter predictions can be wrong. Neurons have no activity at rest, so inhibition of a silent neuron has no effect. Absolute firing rates are not reliable; changes in rate are more reliable.

Can it walk by itself?

No. The legs on this page move by a trained policy. We activated DNg100, a descending neuron that starts walking in real flies, at 50–200 Hz on BANC. About 80–110 of the 393 leg motor neurons fire, with no rhythm, and flexors and extensors of a joint fire together. A leg would stiffen, not step. We then activated each of the 473 types of descending neuron in turn. 169 types make leg motor neurons fire. None makes a rhythm clearly above random firing, and none makes flexors and extensors alternate.

Pugliese et al. found a walking rhythm in a rate model of the nerve cord [12]. We wrote their model again from their Methods and ran it on BANC (tools/rate_vnc.py). Their result holds: with DNg100 driven, all six legs' networks make a rhythm at 8–11 Hz in 8 of 8 runs at a moderate input, and most runs stop with stronger input. We sent this rhythm to flybody's legs, tethered (tools/leg_drive.py). Most feet move by less than 0.1 mm, one hind foot rises 0.27 mm, and the middle legs almost do not move.

Real flies set a clear test. Sapkal et al. activated DNg100 in flies with no head and little leg feedback. All six legs stepped, left and right alternated, and the legs kept a tripod [13]. The model on BANC does not do this (tools/phase.py, input 90). Muscles that swing a front leg forward and back are about 45–75° apart, not 180°. The front legs are within about 35° of each other, not 180°. We tested the input that gives the clearest rhythm, and there is no tripod. So the wiring holds part of what walking needs, but this model does not walk from it.

Smell fails too. A real fly turns toward the antenna that smells more, through DNa02 [14]. On FlyWire, olfactory input usually ignites the whole brain or does nothing, and DNa02 fires on the fly's left whichever antenna gets the input (tools/intent.py steer). Silencing Kenyon cells, excitatory antennal lobe neurons or all neuromodulator neurons, or weakening all synapses, did not help, and neither does adaptation. It may be an error in the model, not in the fly.

The jump does not reach the muscles. Looming neurons drive the giant fiber, but it does not drive the jump muscle's motor neuron: in the fly that synapse is mostly electrical, and the connectome has chemical synapses only. So a threshold on the giant fiber's rate starts the jump.

What is wrong with this fly

  • It smells nothing and sees nothing. Odor input ignites the model (see Can it walk by itself?), and visual input from photoreceptors does not reach the looming neurons in this model (tools/vision_reach.py). The fly knows where the sugar is because we tell it.
  • Landing has no brain. We looked for a landing command. LPLC4 drives DNp07, but about the same from one eye (67 / 0 Hz) as from two (74 / 74 Hz), and that does not pass the test we set in advance (tools/land.py). So a rule decides.
  • Hunger is one number. Thirst, sleep, mating, grooming and learning are absent.
  • A flat world. The fly cannot walk on the fruit or land on the mug, because the walking policy knows only flat ground. Its goals and paths go around what stands there. It sometimes clips an edge: in those six runs, 1.8 s of 30 minutes was inside something.
  • No feeling of its own body. The legs, wings and halteres send no input to the model.
